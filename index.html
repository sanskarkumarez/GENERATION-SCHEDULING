Optimal Scheduling of Mixed Energy Sources

Forecast tomorrow’s load, schedule solar, wind, hydro, coal and gas around it, then predict which hours will break a constraint. Built end to end in Python, and every number below comes from the code in this repo.

All data is synthetic: two years of hourly load, solar and wind that I generated with weekday, Sunday, holiday, temperature and EV-charging effects, so I could control the ground truth. Plant sizes and prices are illustrative. See Limitations below.

The pipeline

Generate
2 years of hourly load, solar, wind, with festival days and a growing EV evening peak.
Forecast
ARIMA, weekly SARIMA, and a hybrid: K-means groups similar days, then ARIMA runs inside the day’s cluster.
Dispatch
Day-ahead LP: cheapest mix under capacity, ramp-rate and hydro-energy limits.
Real time
Replay actual load and renewables. Redispatch within ramp limits, then reserve, then unserved.
Flag
A Random Forest scores each hour’s risk of a breach, and reserve is booked only where it fires.

1. Forecasting: where ARIMA breaks

Plain ARIMA treats every day as the same kind of day. Forecasts below are one-day-ahead over the held-out Sep to Dec 2024 period (4 clusters, chosen by silhouette score), which includes Dussehra and Diwali.

MAPE, lower is better. Pick Sun or Holidays: ARIMA and even weekly SARIMA fail on festival days (about 28 to 29%), while clustering similar days cuts it to about 5.1%. Naive means “same hour last week”.

3. Flagging constraint breaches

Label: an hour counts as a breach if real-time deviations exceed what ramp-limited headroom plus a 2% reserve can cover. Features are all known day-ahead: forecasts, scheduled headroom and gas use, net-load ramp, time of day, temperature, cluster, recent error and the disagreement between the two forecasters. Trained on Mar to Aug, tested on Sep to Dec.

What the forest looks at

Scheduled headroom, gas dispatch and net load dominate, which matches the physics: breaches happen when the schedule leaves little ramping room at the evening peak. Precision is modest, so this is a screening tool, not an alarm.

4. Does flagging pay off?

Four operating policies replayed over the same 122 held-out days. Reserve is a contracted flexible resource priced at ₹9,000/MWh when used and ₹400/MW per hour to hold.

  • Better forecasting (A to B) lowers cost by about 1.3% but barely changes unserved energy, which suggests the remaining breaches are driven by capacity and ramp limits more than by forecast error (I have not isolated this rigorously).
  • RF-targeted reserve (C) cuts unserved energy by 18.1% against B and 17.4% against A, and costs 1.6% less than A overall.
  • Blanket 8% reserve (D) buys a bit more security but books 166,203 MW-h of reserve against 55,173 for C, and ends up more expensive than C. C captures about 82.7% of D’s unserved-energy reduction.

Honest result: the total cost saving is small (1.6% vs the ARIMA baseline), far below the 10 to 15% I originally targeted. Fuel cost dominates the total, and the real gain is in security of supply per rupee of reserve.

What broke along the way

  • The grid was too comfortable. My first capacities gave only 0.16% of hours with a breach, so there was nothing for the classifier to learn. I tightened thermal and hydro capacity until about 4% of hours were stressed.
  • The dispatch LP went infeasible. On low-demand mornings, plants could not ramp down fast enough from the previous evening’s output to match net load. I traced it by printing the net load against the ramp-down floor, then added an over-generation slack (exports or backing down, priced at ₹2,000/MWh).
  • ARIMA failed on Sundays and festivals. Its error on Sundays was more than 4× its weekday error, and the weekly SARIMA fix still missed festival days. Clustering days by load shape and routing each forecast day to its cluster fixed both.
  • The oracle row was misleading. A perfect-forecast comparison looked worse than my real policies on unserved energy, because my real-time model checks each hour on its own without carrying ramps between hours. I removed that row rather than present a number I do not trust.

Limitations

  • Synthetic data and illustrative prices. Next step: rerun on a public load dataset (for example POSOCO or ENTSO-E) and a real wind and solar series.
  • The scheduler is an LP relaxation. There are no unit commitment binaries, minimum stable generation or start-up costs.
  • The real-time stage is hour by hour and ignores ramp coupling between hours, so it is optimistic. It also treats the contracted reserve as always available.
  • One random seed and one train/test split, so the cost differences between policies are small enough that I would not call them statistically settled.
  • Python only: statsmodels, scikit-learn and SciPy (HiGHS). A Simulink model of the same dispatch is future work.
